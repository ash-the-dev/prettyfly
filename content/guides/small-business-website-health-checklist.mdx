A small-business website health routine should cover uptime, forms, SSL, backups, broken links, search visibility, analytics, mobile usability, performance, accessibility, and account ownership. Some checks should run continuously. Others belong in a weekly, monthly, or quarterly review.

The point is not to stare at dashboards all day. It is to catch quiet failures before they cost leads, trust, rankings, or sales.

## Daily checks

Daily checks should be quick. Confirm the site loads, key pages are reachable, and no critical alerts appeared overnight. If your website produces leads or sales, check that yesterday's conversions look plausible.

- Homepage loads
- Primary CTA works
- No uptime alert
- No obvious analytics drop
- No critical form complaint

## Weekly checks

Weekly checks are for patterns. Review search visibility, top landing pages, form submissions, broken links, and speed on important templates. Look for changes that are large enough to matter.

This is also a good moment to check recently published pages. Are they indexed? Are internal links present? Do titles and descriptions make sense?

## Monthly checks

Monthly checks should include deeper reviews of analytics, Search Console, content performance, outdated pages, accessibility basics, and technical warnings. You are looking for drift: things that slowly got worse while everyone was busy.

Document what you changed. A simple log prevents the classic mystery where traffic changed, nobody remembers the deployment, and every theory sounds equally possible.

## Quarterly checks

Quarterly reviews are for ownership and resilience. Confirm domain renewal, DNS access, hosting access, CMS accounts, backup restoration, plugin or dependency updates, and who owns each vendor relationship.

Small businesses often discover access problems during emergencies. That is the worst possible time to learn nobody knows where the domain lives.

## Uptime, forms, and SSL

Uptime monitoring tells you whether the site is reachable. Form testing tells you whether visitors can contact you. SSL checks tell you whether browsers can trust the connection. These are basic, but basic failures are still expensive.

Test forms like a real visitor. Submit a message, confirm validation works, check the recipient inbox, and make sure spam protection does not block legitimate users.

## Backups and broken links

Backups only matter if you can restore them. Review backup frequency, storage location, access, and restoration steps. For broken links, check internal links, redirects, deleted pages, and important external references.

A broken link from a high-intent page to a missing contact page is not a tiny technical issue. It is a leak in the path to revenue.

## Search visibility and analytics

Search Console helps you see impressions, clicks, indexing issues, and query changes. Analytics helps you see what visitors do after they arrive. Neither tool is perfect alone.

Review them together. A drop in clicks may be an SEO issue, but it may also coincide with downtime, slow pages, a deployment, or broken tracking.

## Mobile usability, performance, and accessibility

Open the site on a real phone. Check tap targets, navigation, forms, readability, sticky elements, and page speed. Then use automated tools as a supplement, not a replacement for human review.

Accessibility checks should include headings, labels, contrast, keyboard navigation, alt text, and visible focus states. These basics help more people use the site and often improve overall clarity.

## Ownership and account access

Keep a private inventory of domain registrar, DNS, hosting, CMS, analytics, Search Console, email, form tools, and payment accounts. Include owners, recovery emails, renewal dates, and two-factor authentication status.

Do not put secrets in a shared doc. Store credentials in a password manager and make sure more than one trusted person can recover critical access.

## What to do next

Create a recurring checklist with daily, weekly, monthly, and quarterly sections. Automate uptime and critical alerts, then schedule a monthly review for the broader issues humans should judge.
